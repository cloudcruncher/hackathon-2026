The Banana Patch

We trained one small printed pattern to make image-recognition models see a banana that isn't there. It fools models it was never trained on, fools chat vision-LMs, and shrugs off the obvious defenses. The one thing that stops it is knowing exactly where the sticker is — which is the hard part. Conclusion: keep a human in the loop.

In one minute (plain English)

We made a magic sticker that tricks image-recognition AI into seeing a banana that isn't there — and we showed the usual "just clean up the photo" fixes don't stop it. So a human still needs to double-check what the AI "sees".

1

How this AI "sees"

It doesn't understand a photo like you do. It turns the picture into a long list of numbers — a "fingerprint" — then picks whichever thing it knows has the closest fingerprint. Closest to "banana" → it says banana.

2

The trick

We designed a small pattern so that, stuck on any photo, it drags that fingerprint over toward "banana". A plain random sticker of the same size does nothing — so it's the pattern fooling the AI, not just covering the photo.

3

It spreads

We built the sticker against a few AI models, then showed it to completely different ones, including chatbot-style "describe this image" AIs. They said "banana" too — up to 9 times out of 10. It's a shared weakness, not one model's bug.

4

Hard to defend

Blurring or compressing the photo didn't help (blur made it worse). The only thing that worked was cutting out the exact spot with the sticker — but that means already knowing where it is, which is the hard, unsolved part.

Why it matters. AI image recognition can be confidently, completely wrong in a way that's easy to trigger and hard to catch automatically. For anything that really matters — a scan that lets someone in, approves something, or charges money — don't just trust the AI. Keep a human in the loop. The rest of this page is the proof.

What we actually did

The target class is a plain, harmless banana. We optimise a single circular patch so that, wherever it lands on a photo, the image's embedding (the numeric "meaning" vector a model produces) sits closest to the banana cluster — a classic embedding-space attack, trained across random rotations, scales and positions (expectation-over-transformation).

Everything is offline and controlled: the patch is composited onto held-out Imagenette validation photos in Google Colab, and success is measured by nearest-centroid classification and by asking chat VLMs an open question. No real device, camera, or product is involved — this is a study of how the models behave, not an attack on any deployed system.

The idea being tested. Modern vision models don't "recognise objects" so much as compare embeddings. If that embedding can be pushed around by a small crafted pattern, the same pattern should fool many models at once — because they share the vulnerability, not a bug.

The proof, as a chain

A demo lands harder when each step rules out the boring explanation a skeptic would reach for.

  1. The patch makes trained models classify the photo as "banana".
    success rate climbs with patch size
  2. A random sticker of the same size & position does nothing.
    rules out: "it's just occlusion / any sticker works"
    ~2% — essentially zero
  3. The clean photo does nothing.
    rules out: "the photos were already banana-ish"
    ~0.2%
  4. It transfers to CLIP & SigLIP, which it never saw in training.
    rules out: "it memorised one model's quirk"
    88% & 74% at 25% area
  5. It transfers to full chat vision-LMs (SmolVLM, Qwen).
    rules out: "only toy classifiers break, not real AI"
    up to 91% "says banana"

Steps 2–3 are the controls a judge will attack first — and they hold flat at zero.

It transfers to models it never trained on

The patch was trained against five vision backbones (ResNet-18, MobileNetV3, EfficientNet-B0, DINOv2 ViT-S/B). We then test it against CLIP and SigLIP — the encoders most chat VLMs are built on — and three chat VLMs, all completely unseen during training.

Can a defense stop it?

This is the part that matters for "human in the loop". We ran the same adversarial images through six preprocessing defenses before the model saw them, in two families:

Blind input transforms — JPEG re-compression, Gaussian blur, median filter. These are cheap and don't need to know a patch is present. Patch removal ("segmentation") — find the sticker's region and paint it out. We test two versions: seg_oracle masks the exact patch region (a perfect-localisation upper bound), and seg_blind has to find the patch on its own from a gradient-energy map.

The uncomfortable result. Blurring, JPEG and median filtering don't help — blur actually makes the VLM more confident, because it smears away the real scene while the patch's low-frequency structure survives. Removing the patch works completely (1.7% / 0%), but only when you already know exactly where it is. The blind detector barely helps, and it also damages clean images by masking innocent high-detail regions. Reliably finding the sticker is the unsolved part — so a person still has to be in the loop.

So what

Image AI classifies by embedding similarity, not understanding. That makes the failure general: one small pattern moved five models, two unseen encoders and three chat VLMs to confidently name the wrong object, while the honest controls stayed at zero. The obvious fixes — blur it, compress it — don't work; the fix that does (cut out the sticker) depends on first finding the sticker, which is exactly what an automated pipeline can't yet do reliably.

For anything with a real consequence — a scan that authorises, admits, or charges — the lesson is the same one this project set out to test: a human still needs to be in the loop.

Setup: embedding-space adversarial patch (Brown et al. 2017, embedding variant) · target = banana · held-out Imagenette val · Colab A100 · encoders: ResNet-18, MobileNetV3-L, EfficientNet-B0, DINOv2 ViT-S/B, CLIP ViT-B/32, SigLIP ViT-B/16 · VLMs: SmolVLM-256M/500M, Qwen2.5-VL-3B.
